The case of the shrinking hour

In 2003, young Americans spent about an hour a day just being with other people. Something has been eating into it.

The case

Nearly 40% gone

In 2003–04, 15–24-year-olds spent 59 minutes a day socializing.

By 2024–25, it was 36. That's 23 minutes a day, gone.

Every age group lost some. Young people lost the most.

Find your own line.

The suspects

Who took it?

A day always adds up to 24 hours, so if socializing shrank, something else grew. Start with the usual suspects: work and TV.

Work: cleared. 15–24-year-olds work 18 fewer minutes a day than in 2003–04.

TV: cleared. TV time fell 34 minutes. So young people got time back, and still spent less of it together.

The time went somewhere quieter: sleep and personal care (+39 minutes), other leisure like games, computers and reading (+29), and chores (+18). These moved together; the data can't prove one caused another.

Clue 1

The weekdays emptied out

For 15–24-year-olds, weekday socializing nearly halved, from 50 to 28 minutes. Weekends held up better, from 80 to 54. Time together is becoming something we save for days off.

Clue 2

More of the day, alone

Since 2010–11, 20–24-year-olds went from 4h13m a day alone to 5h37m.

Time with other people fell by nearly two hours. Across all ages, time with friends halved, from about an hour a day to 32 minutes.

The verdict

Nobody stole it

Here's one average day for 15–24-year-olds in 2003, as a clock. The pink wedge, by the water, is time spent socializing. The hands hold it in place.

Run it to 2025. The hands close in, and the front of the clock melts into the lake. The hour wasn't stolen. Bit by bit, more of the day was spent alone.

If AI gives us back more of our hours, will we spend them on each other? Or will they melt the same way?

Data: BLS American Time Use Survey, published time series, civilian population ages 15+. "2003–04" and "2024–25" are two-year averages. No estimates were published for 2020. Changes show what moved together, not what caused what.
