The case of the missing hour

In 2003, Americans aged 15–24 spent about an hour a day with other people. Today it's 36 minutes. Same 24 hours. So where did the time go?

The case

An hour goes missing

In 2003–04, 15–24-year-olds spent 59 minutes a day socializing.

By 2024–25, it was 36 minutes. Nearly 40% of it, gone.

Every age group lost some. Young people lost the most.

Find your own line.

The suspects

Who took it?

A day always adds up to 24 hours. If socializing shrank, something else grew. Start with the usual suspects.

Work: cleared. 15–24-year-olds work 18 fewer minutes a day than in 2003–04.

TV: cleared. TV time fell 34 minutes.

The trail leads somewhere quieter. Sleep and personal care grew 39 minutes. Other leisure, like games, computers, reading and sports, grew 29. Chores grew 18. These moved together; the data can't prove one caused another.

Clue 1

The weekdays emptied out

For 15–24-year-olds, weekday socializing nearly halved, from 50 to 28 minutes. Weekends held up better, from 80 to 54. Time together is becoming something we save for days off.

Clue 2

More of the day, alone

Since 2010–11, 20–24-year-olds went from 4h13m a day alone to 5h37m.

Time with other people fell by nearly two hours. Across all ages, time with friends halved, from about an hour a day to 32 minutes.

The verdict

Nobody stole it

This is one average day for 15–24-year-olds in 2003, as a clock. Each wedge is an activity's share of the day. The glowing one is socializing.

Run it to 2025. The day didn't get busier. It went soft, and time with other people was the part that melted.

If AI gives us back more of our hours, will we spend them on each other? Or will they melt the same way?

Data: BLS American Time Use Survey, published time series, civilian population ages 15+. "2003–04" and "2024–25" are two-year averages. No estimates were published for 2020. Changes show what moved together, not what caused what.
