The case of the shrinking hour

In 2003, young Americans spent about an hour a day just being with other people. Something has been eating into it.

The case

Nearly 40% gone

In 2003–04, 15–24-year-olds spent 59 minutes a day socializing.

By 2024–25, it was 36. That's 23 minutes a day, gone.

Every age group lost some. Young people lost the most.

Find your own line.

The constraint

A day can't grow

Twenty-four hours. Fourteen hundred and forty minutes, every one of them written down. If socializing lost 23 minutes, those 23 minutes are sitting somewhere else in this column.

Watch 2003 become 2025. Four blocks do almost all the moving: sleep and personal care up 39 minutes, other leisure up 29, chores up 18, TV down 34.

So the question was never where did the hour go. It's who was holding it.

The suspects

Round up the usual

If young people stopped seeing each other, the easy story is that they got busier. Two suspects: the job, and the screen.

Work: cleared. 15–24-year-olds work 18 fewer minutes a day than in 2003–04.

TV: cleared. TV fell 34 minutes. Both suspects gave young people time back — and they still spent less of it together.

The suspects

Then it has to be the kids

A small child eats a day whole. If anyone lost a social life in twenty years, it should be parents.

These numbers cover everyone 15 and older, whichever group you pick above. BLS doesn't publish parent status by age.

Cleared, and then some. Parents of a child under 18 lost 6 minutes a day. People with no kids at home lost 13.

Weekends make it starker. Parents kept almost all of their weekend socializing — down 4 minutes. People with no kids lost 17.

Whatever took the hour, it took most of it from the people with the fewest obligations.

The suspects

One suspect didn't walk

Of the fourteen blocks in the day, one more than doubled: playing games and using a computer for fun. For 20–24-year-olds, 29 minutes a day in 2003–04. Sixty now.

Here's where we stop. The survey records what you were doing, not what you would have done instead. Two lines moved in opposite directions over twenty years. That is the whole claim.

Clue 1

The weekdays emptied out

For 15–24-year-olds, weekday socializing nearly halved, from 50 to 28 minutes. Weekends held up better, from 80 to 54. Time together is becoming something we save for days off.

Clue 2

The day went quiet

Since 2010–11, 20–24-year-olds went from 4h13m a day alone to 5h37m. Time with other people fell by nearly two hours.

The pandemic shows up exactly where you'd expect: alone time peaked in 2021, at 6h44m. Then it came down — and stopped well above where it started.

Clue 3

The empty chair

Across all ages, time with friends fell from 65 minutes a day in 2010 to 32 in 2024–25.

These numbers cover all ages 15 and older, whichever group you pick above. Time with friends includes activities beyond socializing.

And it was going before anything closed. By 2019 it was already down to 43 minutes. The decline was underway before the pandemic.

There is no annual estimate for 2020. By 2021, time with friends was 34 minutes a day. The missing year is a gap in the survey, not a day with nobody there.

In 2024–25, the average was 32 minutes. Each figure stands for about one minute of an average day, not one person followed through the years.

Clue 3

Not everyone left. Time with a spouse or partner didn't move — 158 minutes, then 157. Family held. Coworkers fell by 40 minutes. The people we lose are the ones we have to choose.

These numbers cover all ages 15 and older, whichever group you pick above.

The verdict

Nobody stole it

Here's one average day for 15–24-year-olds in 2003, as a clock. The pink wedge, by the water, is time spent socializing. The hands hold it in place.

Run it to 2025. The hands close in, and the front of the clock melts into the lake. The hour wasn't stolen. Bit by bit, more of the day was spent alone.

Case closed. But a verdict isn't an explanation — so here's what the file actually says.

The closing

What the file says

No suspect took the hour. Work gave time back. TV gave time back. Parents, who have the least room in a day, kept the most of their social lives. The hour wasn't stolen by one thief — it dissolved into a day that is easier and easier to spend alone.

These are changes in averages, not evidence that any one activity caused the decline.

In July 2026, the Bureau of Labor Statistics proposed adding questions about AI use to this survey, with collection proposed for January 2027. That notice sought approval; it did not announce a final decision. If approved, the questions could help researchers examine AI use alongside daily time use.

Methods and notes

What is in the file

The figures are survey averages. They describe how groups' days changed, not what happened to the same people over twenty years.

Source and population
The BLS American Time Use Survey describes the U.S. civilian noninstitutional population ages 15 and older. This site uses published annual time series for 2003–2025, converted from hours to minutes in our extraction script and stored in the shared data file. See the ATUS User's Guide.
What counts as socializing?
The socializing series is BLS “socializing and communicating,” activity code 600024: face-to-face social communication and social functions. Phone calls, texting, and email are classified separately. It is different from “time with friends,” which can include other activities done in a friend's company. See BLS concepts and definitions.
Annual values and two-year averages
Labels such as 2003–04 and 2024–25 are arithmetic means of the two annual estimates in our file. They are not a new calculation from pooled microdata. The friends pictogram begins with 2010 alone (64.8 min/day) and ends with the 2024–25 mean (31.5). Choosing 2025 alone shows 33. Labels round for readability; inspection text keeps one decimal.
A fixed day, with a small rounding adjustment
The day column contains fourteen mutually exclusive display categories. Other leisure and sports is total leisure minus socializing and TV, so these are not counted twice. Published rounding makes both endpoint totals 1,440.3 minutes. Block heights are scaled by 1,440 divided by the reported total; the minute labels are unchanged. Positions between the endpoints are illustrative blends, not observed years or a timetable of activities.
Why 2020 is missing
BLS suspended collection for part of spring 2020 and did not publish a comparable annual estimate. We leave that year missing; it is neither zero nor an interpolated observation. See BLS's explanation of the 2020 gap.
Who-with measures and age bands
The companion series in this extract starts in 2010. It covers activities for which ATUS records companions, not another complete 24-hour budget. Companion categories can overlap and must not be added together. Friends uses all ages; several alone/together views use 20–24 rather than 15–24 because the published age bands differ. Each view states its group.
Day types and parent groups
Weekdays are nonholiday weekdays; weekends include holidays. Parent groups mean people with or without an own child under 18 at home. Our published-series extract does not cross age with parent status, so it cannot isolate young parents or provide an age-by-parent filter.
What the evidence can—and cannot—say
Activities moved together; the survey does not identify which activity displaced another or establish that screens, the pandemic, or AI caused a change. Subgroup estimates have sampling uncertainty; small year-to-year differences should not be treated as precise individual changes.
Figures are units, not tracked people
Each filled figure in the friends view represents approximately one minute per day, rounded to the nearest minute. Empty chairs compare that reading with the 2010 baseline. The same respondents were not followed across years, and the illustration does not show the number of friends a person has.
