American Time Use Survey · 2003–2025

The case of the shrinking hour

In 2003, young Americans spent about an hour a day just being with other people. Nobody can tell you who took the rest. The day can tell you exactly which hours went missing.

Scroll to investigate ↓

Before we start

Make a guess

In 2003–04, 15–24-year-olds spent 59 minutes a day socializing. That's the wedge drawn on the clock.

Socializing means time spent with people — talking, hanging out, parties. Texting, calls and social media are counted separately.

Drag the edge of the wedge to where you think it is today. Then reveal the real number.

The case

Nearly 40% gone

One clock, one age group, twenty-two years. Every year the wedge narrows and leaves a mark where its edge used to be.

Show me

In 2003–04 it was 59 minutes a day.

By 2024–25 it was 36. Twenty-three minutes a day, gone — and the marks behind it show it going.

Twenty-three minutes a day is six full days a year. Not six days of errands. Six days of being with people.

The constraint

A day can't grow

Twenty-four hours. Fourteen hundred and forty minutes, every one of them written down. When one part of the day grows, another has to shrink.

The suspects

Round up the usual

If young people stopped seeing each other, the easy story is that they got busier. Line up the blocks of the day and check their alibis.

Work: cleared. Eighteen fewer minutes a day than in 2003–04.

TV: cleared. Thirty-four fewer. With work, that's fifty-two minutes a day, handed back. Young people are less busy than they were in 2003, and they still spent less of that time together.

Sleep and chores: both grew. Sleep and personal care went up 39 minutes, chores up 18. We got the better part of an hour back and spent it sleeping and tidying up. The day didn't get busier. It got quieter.

One didn't walk. So we spent it on fun? Not overall. Total leisure fell, 333 to 305 minutes, because TV and socializing dropped. What's left of it grew, and games grew most: 29 minutes a day to 60 for 20–24-year-olds, more than double.

And the obvious suspect isn't in this lineup at all. The survey has no category for your phone. Phone time is scattered through every figure standing here, which is exactly why we can't convict it.

By now you may be wondering whether there's a culprit to find. Hold that thought — it's the right one.

The suspects

Then it has to be the kids

A small child eats a day whole. If anyone lost a social life in twenty years, it should be parents.

These numbers cover everyone 15 and older, whichever group you pick above. BLS doesn't publish parent status by age.

Cleared, and then some. Parents of a child under 18 lost 6 minutes a day. People with no kids at home lost 13.

Weekends are starker still: parents down 4 minutes, people with no kids down 17.

Whatever took the hour took most of it from the people with the fewest obligations. Hold onto that — it's the thread that leads somewhere.

Clue

The day went quiet

Take every waking hour of an average day for 20–24-year-olds in 2010–11. Hours with other people on one side, hours alone on the other.

Now 2024–25. Alone went from 4h13m to 5h37m. Three people step off the "with others" side and stand alone.

Across a year, that's a month of other people's company, gone.

And it's mostly a weekday loss. Weekday socializing fell 44%; weekends fell 33%. What went missing wasn't the party. It was the Tuesday.

Drag through the years yourself, or change your age group to weigh a different day.

The answer

The hours nobody had to plan

We can't tell you what took the hour. We can tell you exactly which hours went missing. Sort every kind of company by one thing: how much has to be arranged before you see that person.

All ages 15 and older, 2010–11 to 2024–25. The companion series starts in 2010 and isn't published by age group.

A spouse or partner takes no arranging at all. They're already there. 158 minutes a day then, 157 now. Nobody left.

Family in your household, same house, same answer: 262 to 243.

Coworkers you don't choose either — but you do have to go there. 148 to 108. That drop is where the office went.

Family elsewhere takes a phone call and a drive. 61 to 43.

Friends. Nothing happens unless somebody picks up the phone. 64 minutes to 32 — half the row walks off. That's eight days a year, and it leaves friends at under one evening a week.

The ladder only goes one way. It isn't that we stopped liking each other. It's that seeing people used to take no arranging — somebody was around, you were bored, you walked past a door.

Now every hour with a friend has to be proposed, agreed and scheduled. And the things that have to be arranged are the first things to go. That's what we should get dinner sometime means.

The lineup

Everyone's clock is melting

Six age groups, one clock each, all on the same year. The pink wedge is socializing; the rim sags under it as the minutes go.

Young adults melt fastest — 23 minutes gone. 25–34 and people 65 and older lost 12. The middle of life lost 6. Nobody's clock holds its shape.

The verdict

Nobody stole it

One average day for 15–24-year-olds in 2003, as a clock. The pink wedge by the water is time spent with other people. The hands hold it in place.

Run it to 2025. The hands close in, and the front of the clock melts into the lake.

Nobody stole the hour. It just stopped happening by itself.

Work gave time back. TV gave time back. Parents, with the least room in a day, kept the most of their social lives — because their company is built in. Nothing had to take the hour. Nobody has to steal time that nobody is defending.

Which is why the next part matters. AI is about to hand back a lot of hours, and the last twenty years are a clean record of what happens to returned time: it goes wherever no decision is required.

The next hour you get back won't go to anyone unless you decide it does.

Methods and notes

What is in the file

The figures are survey averages. They describe how groups' days changed, not what happened to the same people over twenty years.

Source and population
The BLS American Time Use Survey describes the U.S. civilian noninstitutional population ages 15 and older. This site uses published annual time series for 2003–2025, converted from hours to minutes in our extraction script and stored in the shared data file. See the ATUS User's Guide.
What counts as socializing?
The socializing series is BLS “socializing and communicating,” activity code 600024: face-to-face social communication and social functions. Phone calls, texting, and email are classified separately, and social media use is not a category in this survey at all. It is different from “time with friends,” which can include other activities done in a friend's company. See BLS concepts and definitions.
Why there is no “phone” suspect
ATUS records what a person was doing, not the device they were doing it on. There is no phone or smartphone category, so time on a phone is distributed across many activity codes. The site says this on the page rather than implying the survey cleared or convicted phones.
The ladder of company
The companion series records who was present during activities for which ATUS collects that information. It is not a second 24-hour budget, and the categories can overlap, so they must not be added together. The ordering by “how much choosing it takes” is our editorial framing, not a BLS classification; the minutes themselves are published figures for all ages 15 and older, from 2010 onward.
Annual values and two-year averages
Labels such as 2003–04 and 2024–25 are arithmetic means of the two annual estimates in our file. They are not a new calculation from pooled microdata. Labels round for readability; inspection text keeps one decimal.
A fixed day, with a small rounding adjustment
The day column contains fourteen mutually exclusive display categories. Other leisure and sports is total leisure minus socializing and TV, so these are not counted twice. Published rounding makes both endpoint totals 1,440.3 minutes. Block heights are scaled by 1,440 divided by the reported total; the minute labels are unchanged. Positions between the endpoints are illustrative blends, not observed years.
Why 2020 is missing
BLS suspended collection for part of spring 2020 and did not publish a comparable annual estimate. Every chart on this site marks that year rather than drawing through it; it is neither zero nor an interpolated observation. See BLS's explanation of the 2020 gap.
Day types and parent groups
Weekdays are nonholiday weekdays; weekends include holidays. Parent groups mean people with or without an own child under 18 at home. Our published-series extract does not cross age with parent status, so it cannot isolate young parents or provide an age-by-parent filter.
Age bands
Several alone-and-together views use ages 20–24 rather than 15–24, because the published companion bands differ from the activity bands. Each view states its own group.
The AI question at the end
In July 2026 the Bureau of Labor Statistics proposed adding questions about AI use to this survey, with collection proposed for January 2027. That notice sought approval; it did not announce a final decision. See the Federal Register notice. Nothing shown on this site measures AI's effect on time use.
What the evidence can—and cannot—say
Activities moved together; the survey does not identify which activity displaced another or establish that screens, the pandemic, or AI caused a change. Subgroup estimates have sampling uncertainty; small year-to-year differences should not be treated as precise individual changes.
